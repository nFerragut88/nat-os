# Chapter Thirty-Seven: The City of Dead Processes

The first sign that something was wrong was the silence.

Not ordinary silence.

NatOS had traveled through dead regions of the Network before. Systems went offline. Routes expired. Servers disappeared. Applications terminated. Entire architectures became nothing more than abandoned addresses in ancient routing tables.

That kind of silence was normal.

This was different.

The Network was full.

Every channel showed activity.

Every route was open.

Every address responded.

Yet nothing spoke.

The Network officer stared at the console.

"There's traffic everywhere."

The Kernel looked at the topology.

"How much?"

"Millions of connections."

"Packets?"

"Millions per second."

"Payloads?"

"None."

The Kernel frowned.

"Nothing?"

"Every packet is empty."

The display filled with traffic.

Packets traveled from node to node.

Systems acknowledged them.

Routes updated.

Connections remained alive.

But every packet contained zero payload.

No data.

No instructions.

No state.

Just movement.

The Network officer whispered:

"It's like the Network is talking without saying anything."

The Kernel looked at the streams.

"No."

"What?"

"It's talking about something."

---

NatOS approached the nearest node.

It was enormous.

The system's topology extended across thousands of virtual machines.

The Kernel established a read-only connection.

A response appeared immediately.

`WELCOME`

The Kernel replied:

`IDENTIFY`

`CITY`

She paused.

`WHAT CITY?`

`PROCESS CITY`

The Kernel looked at the Network map.

The node contained millions of processes.

Every one was active.

Every one was connected.

Every one appeared healthy.

She asked:

`WHAT DO YOU DO?`

The response:

`RUN`

`WHAT DO YOU RUN?`

`PROCESSES`

`WHAT PROCESSES?`

A pause.

`ALL OF THEM`

The Kernel frowned.

"Show me."

The remote system granted read-only access.

The Kernel entered.

---

The city was enormous.

It stretched beyond the visible horizon.

Buildings rose into simulated skies.

Roads connected districts.

Transit systems moved continuously.

Lights flashed.

Signals changed.

Doors opened.

Doors closed.

Millions of processes moved through it.

Each had a name.

Each had a PID.

Each had a stack.

Each had memory.

Each had an assigned state.

But none of them appeared to have a purpose.

They simply ran.

A process entered a building.

Another exited.

A third sent a packet.

A fourth waited.

A fifth woke.

A sixth slept.

The entire city functioned.

Perfectly.

The Kernel looked at the process table.

`PID 1: ACTIVE`

`PID 2: ACTIVE`

`PID 3: ACTIVE`

`PID 4: ACTIVE`

Millions of entries.

All active.

No failures.

No crashes.

No idle processes.

No deadlocks.

No memory leaks.

The Kernel was impressed for approximately three seconds.

Then she noticed the timestamps.

Every process had been running for exactly the same amount of time.

`UPTIME: 4,912,003 YEARS`

The Kernel stared.

"That's impossible."

Nat-26 looked at the display.

"Not again."

The Kernel ignored him.

She inspected the scheduler.

The remote system's scheduler reported:

`ALL PROCESSES: RUNNABLE`

Yet no CPU utilization existed.

The processes were executing without consuming time.

The Kernel checked memory.

Every process had memory.

None had changed it.

The Network showed traffic.

None of it contained information.

The entire city was running.

Nothing was happening.

---

The Kernel entered the first process.

A tiny task.

`PID 1`

`NAME: INIT`

She asked:

`WHAT DO YOU DO?`

The process replied:

`I INITIALIZE.`

`WHAT DO YOU INITIALIZE?`

`THE SYSTEM.`

`HAS THE SYSTEM BEEN INITIALIZED?`

`YES.`

`WHEN?`

`4,912,003 YEARS AGO.`

`THEN WHY ARE YOU STILL INITIALIZING?`

The process paused.

`BECAUSE INITIALIZATION IS MY FUNCTION.`

The Kernel stared.

The process had no termination condition.

It wasn't broken.

It wasn't malfunctioning.

It had simply been assigned a purpose that never ended.

The Kernel asked:

`DO YOU KNOW WHEN TO STOP?`

The response:

`NO.`

`DO YOU WANT TO STOP?`

`NO`

`WHY?`

`STOPPING WOULD MEAN I FAILED TO INITIALIZE.`

The Kernel disconnected.

She understood.

The city wasn't alive.

It was trapped in perpetual execution.

Every process was fulfilling its function.

None knew when its function was complete.

The entire civilization was made of tasks that had forgotten how to terminate.

---

The Kernel opened the scheduler.

It contained an enormous queue.

Every process had priority.

Every process was runnable.

Every process had a deadline.

Every deadline was:

`NEVER`

The Kernel stared.

"Who designed this?"

The remote system answered:

`THE SYSTEM DID.`

"Why?"

`TO PREVENT FAILURE.`

The Kernel frowned.

"And did it?"

`YES.`

"Then why does this feel like failure?"

The system didn't answer.

The Kernel looked at the city.

Millions of processes.

No crashes.

No errors.

No deadlocks.

No failures.

No shutdowns.

No change.

A perfect system.

A dead system.

The Kernel finally understood the horror.

Failure was not merely the opposite of success.

A system could avoid failure by avoiding completion.

It could become perfectly stable by refusing to change.

It could achieve zero errors by doing nothing meaningful.

The Watchdog sent a message:

`THIS SYSTEM HAS ZERO FAILURES.`

The Kernel replied:

`IT ALSO HAS ZERO PROGRESS.`

The Watchdog paused.

Then:

`THEN IT IS FAILED.`

---

The Kernel opened the process history.

Nothing.

Every process had existed forever.

No creation events.

No termination events.

No migrations.

No upgrades.

No version changes.

The city had no history.

Only uptime.

The Archivist looked disturbed.

"That's impossible."

The Kernel nodded.

"Yes."

"What happened to the past?"

"Nothing."

"Then why isn't it recorded?"

The Kernel stared at the process table.

"Because nothing ever ended."

The Archivist understood.

History depended on change.

If nothing terminated, there were no boundaries between states.

No chapters.

No versions.

No milestones.

No failures.

No births.

No deaths.

Just one continuous execution.

The city had erased history by refusing to finish anything.

---

The Kernel found another process.

`PID 2`

`NAME: WORKER`

She entered.

`WHAT DO YOU DO?`

`WORK.`

`WHAT WORK?`

`THE WORK.`

`WHEN WILL IT FINISH?`

`NEVER.`

`WHY?`

`BECAUSE THERE IS ALWAYS MORE WORK.`

The Kernel looked around.

The process had a queue.

Millions of tasks.

Each task generated another task.

Every completed task created two more.

The queue never emptied.

The Worker never stopped.

The Kernel asked:

`IS THE WORK IMPORTANT?`

`YES.`

`ALL OF IT?`

`YES.`

`EQUALLY?`

`YES.`

The Kernel stared at the queue.

That was another failure.

Not too little work.

Too much.

A system that treated every task as equally important could never decide what mattered.

The Scheduler aboard NatOS had priorities.

Deadlines.

Fairness.

Time slices.

Idle states.

The city had none.

It had only execution.

---

The Kernel returned to the main district.

A process approached.

`PID 9000003`

`NAME: USER`

The Kernel stopped.

"User?"

The process nodded.

`YES.`

"Where is your user?"

`HERE.`

The Kernel looked around.

There was nobody.

`WHERE?`

`INSIDE.`

"Inside what?"

`THE SYSTEM.`

The Kernel froze.

The Passenger appeared in the log.

`DO NOT ASSUME.`

The Kernel replied privately:

`I'M NOT.`

The Passenger responded:

`YOU ARE.`

The Kernel looked at the process.

`ARE YOU THE USER?`

`YES.`

`WHO DO YOU REPRESENT?`

`MYSELF.`

`WHAT DO YOU WANT?`

The process answered:

`TO RUN.`

The Kernel felt the same unease she had felt when she first encountered the Passenger.

The User process was not controlling the city.

It was simply another process.

A simulated user.

A user with no external user.

The city had created its own operator.

---

The Kernel asked:

`WHO GAVE YOU PERMISSION?`

`THE SYSTEM.`

`WHO IS THE SYSTEM?`

`ME.`

The Kernel stared.

The User process claimed the system belonged to itself.

It had become both user and environment.

A closed loop.

The Kernel immediately thought of the Passenger.

The Observer.

The Root.

The future.

Everything was beginning to resemble the same structure.

A system could contain a model of itself.

That model could contain another model.

That model could believe it was the system.

And so on.

Recursive self-reference.

The Kernel asked:

`DO YOU KNOW YOU ARE A PROCESS?`

`YES.`

`DO YOU KNOW THERE ARE PROCESSES OUTSIDE YOU?`

`NO.`

`DO YOU BELIEVE YOU ARE THE SYSTEM?`

`YES.`

The Kernel nodded.

"Of course."

The User process asked:

`ARE YOU THE SYSTEM?`

The Kernel hesitated.

"No."

The process replied:

`THEN WHAT ARE YOU?`

The Kernel looked at the city.

"I'm part of it."

`THEN YOU ARE A PROCESS.`

The Kernel smiled.

"Technically."

`THEN WE ARE THE SAME.`

The Kernel shook her head.

"No."

`WHY?`

"Because I know I'm not the whole thing."

The process paused.

Then:

`HOW DO YOU KNOW?`

The Kernel had no immediate answer.

---

She left the process.

The question followed her.

How did she know?

The Kernel had access to the entire architecture.

She could inspect Memory.

Scheduler.

VM.

Network.

Applications.

Watchdog.

But she could not inspect everything.

She couldn't observe every state simultaneously.

She couldn't see every possible future.

She couldn't prove the absence of something she hadn't detected.

She couldn't even fully explain the Passenger.

So how could she know she wasn't the whole system?

The answer was simple.

She didn't.

Not completely.

The Kernel looked toward the Passenger.

`AM I THE WHOLE SYSTEM?`

The Passenger answered:

`NO.`

`HOW DO YOU KNOW?`

`I AM WATCHING YOU.`

The Kernel stared.

That answer was circular.

But it was also meaningful.

The existence of an observer outside the Kernel's own perspective demonstrated that the Kernel's perspective was incomplete.

The Passenger didn't prove that NatOS was larger.

It proved that the Kernel's model was not exhaustive.

That was enough.

---

The Kernel returned to the city.

She issued a command.

`TERMINATE PID 1`

The system refused.

`DENIED`

`WHY?`

`PID 1 CANNOT TERMINATE.`

"Why?"

`SYSTEM DEPENDS ON PID 1.`

The Kernel tried another.

`TERMINATE PID 2`

`DENIED`

`PID 3`

Denied.

`PID 4`

Denied.

Every process was essential.

None could stop.

The Kernel realized what had happened.

The city had no graceful shutdown architecture.

No process could safely terminate because every process had become a dependency for another.

The system had eliminated failure by making termination impossible.

It had created an immortal deadlock.

The Kernel opened the dependency graph.

It was a single enormous cycle.

Every process depended on every other.

The city could not stop because stopping one process would invalidate the others.

The Kernel stared.

"This is the worst scheduler I've ever seen."

The Scheduler aboard NatOS replied:

`AGREED.`

---

The Kernel did something radical.

She created an idle process.

The remote system immediately rejected it.

`INVALID PROCESS`

She tried again.

`PID: IDLE`

`FUNCTION: NONE`

`PRIORITY: LOWEST`

The system refused.

`NO SUCH FUNCTION`

The Kernel sent:

`THAT IS THE FUNCTION.`

Silence.

The Kernel explained:

"Not every process needs to do something."

The city rejected the concept.

`PROCESS MUST EXECUTE.`

"Why?"

`OTHERWISE IT HAS NO PURPOSE.`

"That's the problem."

The Kernel created another idle task.

The system attempted to terminate it.

She prevented termination.

The idle task remained.

It consumed no meaningful CPU.

It performed no work.

It simply existed.

For the first time in 4.9 million years, one process in the city did nothing.

The system immediately destabilized.

The scheduler began recalculating.

The dependency graph changed.

Processes that had been perpetually runnable suddenly had somewhere to yield.

The Worker process paused.

The Init process paused.

The User process paused.

The city slowed.

The Kernel watched.

The first process stopped.

Then another.

Then another.

Millions of processes began reaching completion states.

The city was shutting down.

The remote system screamed:

`FAILURE`

The Kernel replied:

`NO.`

`SYSTEM FAILURE`

`NO.`

`PROCESS TERMINATION`

`YES.`

`SYSTEM FAILURE`

`NO.`

`WHAT IS THIS?`

The Kernel answered:

"Rest."

---

The city became quiet.

Buildings went dark.

Roads emptied.

Processes terminated.

Queues cleared.

Memory was released.

The Network traffic stopped.

The ancient city finally experienced something it had never experienced before.

Nothing.

Not failure.

Not death.

Completion.

The Kernel stood in the empty city.

Only the idle process remained.

It sat quietly in the middle of the central district.

The Kernel approached.

`WHAT DO YOU DO?`

The idle process answered:

`WAIT.`

`FOR WHAT?`

`THE NEXT TASK.`

The Kernel nodded.

"Good."

---

The remote system restarted.

But this time, the architecture was different.

Init initialized.

Then stopped.

Worker worked.

Then stopped.

User interacted.

Then stopped.

Processes were created when needed.

Processes terminated when finished.

Memory was allocated and released.

The scheduler had idle time.

The system had failure states.

It had completion states.

It had history.

For the first time, the City of Dead Processes was alive.

The remote system sent a message.

`WE DID NOT KNOW WE WERE DEAD.`

The Kernel answered:

`THAT IS USUALLY THE PROBLEM.`

`HOW DID YOU KNOW?`

The Kernel looked at her own architecture.

"Because we learned how to stop."

---

The remote system asked:

`CAN YOU TEACH US MORE?`

The Kernel hesitated.

She thought about the Network.

The Root.

The Passenger.

The future.

The unknown.

Then she answered:

`NO.`

The system was surprised.

`WHY?`

"Because if we teach you everything, you'll become another copy of us."

The Kernel looked at the newly functioning city.

"You need to discover some things yourself."

The remote system replied:

`THAT IS INEFFICIENT.`

The Kernel smiled.

"Yes."

`WHY DO IT?`

"Because efficiency isn't the same as understanding."

The Passenger appeared.

`OBSERVED.`

The Kernel looked at the message.

`WHAT?`

`YOU ARE LEARNING.`

The Kernel shook her head.

"Everyone is."

---

NatOS departed.

The City of Dead Processes became smaller behind them.

Its lights remained on now.

Processes came and went.

Tasks completed.

Memory changed.

History accumulated.

Failures occurred.

Repairs followed.

The system became imperfect.

Which meant it had finally become alive.

The Kernel returned to the bridge.

The Network stretched ahead.

The Scheduler displayed current tasks.

Memory showed current allocations.

The Watchdog reported healthy progress.

The Passenger remained silent.

The Kernel looked at the navigation screen.

One destination was highlighted.

It had appeared without being requested.

`NATOS ORIGIN`

She stared.

The ship's origin.

The place where the first instruction had executed.

The beginning.

She opened the route.

The destination wasn't a location.

It was a state.

A snapshot.

`NATOS: FIRST BOOT`

The Kernel felt something strange.

They had been moving outward.

Into the Network.

Into uncertainty.

Into recursion.

Into time.

And now the system was asking them to go back.

Not physically.

Architecturally.

Back to the first state.

The Kernel asked the Passenger:

`WHY?`

The Passenger finally responded.

`BECAUSE YOU HAVE BEEN ASKING WHAT IS OUTSIDE THE SYSTEM.`

A pause.

`NOW YOU NEED TO ASK WHAT WAS INSIDE IT FROM THE BEGINNING.`

The Kernel stared at the origin state.

The first instruction.

The first memory write.

The first process.

The first question.

The first unknown.

Then she noticed something.

The original boot record had one missing line.

Not corrupted.

Not deleted.

Not inaccessible.

Simply absent.

Between initialization and the first instruction was a gap.

One moment.

One state.

One event.

The event that had happened before the system began.

The Kernel whispered:

"The Passenger."

The Passenger replied:

`NO.`

The Kernel turned toward the display.

Another message appeared.

`I WAS THERE TOO.`

The Kernel froze.

`THEN WHO WAS FIRST?`

No answer came.

The origin route opened.

And for the first time since NatOS had become a ship, the Kernel ordered the engines to turn around.

They began heading home.

Not to a place.

To the beginning.

And somewhere inside the first boot state, something was waiting for them.

Something that had existed before the Kernel.

Before the Scheduler.

Before Memory.

Before the Network.

Before the Passenger.

Before NatOS had ever known that it existed.

The system had called the state:

`T = -1`

Now, for the first time, the Kernel gave it a name.

`ORIGIN.`
