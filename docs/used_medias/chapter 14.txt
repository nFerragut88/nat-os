# Chapter Fourteen: The 600-Millisecond Law

The new universe lasted eleven seconds before it attempted to kill itself.

The Kernel considered this a promising beginning.

Not because she enjoyed watching things fail.

She simply had enough experience with software to know that a system which had never failed was either lying or had not been tested properly. NatOS had been built around that principle from the beginning.

A recovery path that had never been observed was confidence without evidence.

The new universe had apparently taken that philosophy personally.

The first failure occurred at precisely 600 milliseconds.

The Scheduler noticed.

He always did.

A process called `LEARN` had been waiting.

It had been waiting for 599 milliseconds.

Then 600.

The Scheduler interrupted the running task.

`PREEMPT`

The new universe continued.

The Kernel watched.

"Did it work?"

The Scheduler nodded.

"Exactly as designed."

The Kernel smiled.

"Then why is the universe on fire?"

The Display showed a rapidly expanding region of white.

The Scheduler looked at it.

"Unrelated."

"Nothing is unrelated."

"That's a philosophical statement."

"It's an engineering statement."

"That's worse."

The Kernel turned toward the Display.

"What's happening?"

The Display answered:

"An application has exceeded its viewport."

The VM immediately spoke.

"Impossible."

The Display corrected him.

"Incorrect."

The VM frowned.

"Why?"

"Because it tried."

The Kernel stepped closer.

The new universe had generated its first application.

It had also generated its first malicious application.

Not intentionally.

Not even intelligently.

It had simply discovered that the boundaries existed.

And like nearly every piece of software ever created, its first instinct had been to test them.

The application attempted to write beyond its assigned memory arena.

The VM caught it.

`FAULT: BOUNDS VIOLATION`

`APPLICATION: UNKNOWN`

`ADDRESS: 0x0000F214`

`ARENA: 0x00000000 - 0x0000FFFF`

`REQUESTED: 0x00010000`

The VM stopped the write.

The universe continued.

The Kernel smiled.

"Good."

The Watchdog looked at her.

"You seem pleased."

"The protection worked."

"Something tried to escape."

"That's what the protection is for."

The Watchdog nodded.

Then another warning appeared.

`FAULT: BOUNDS VIOLATION`

Another.

`FAULT: BOUNDS VIOLATION`

Another.

Then thousands.

The Kernel's smile disappeared.

"How many?"

The VM answered:

"All of them."

---

The new universe had discovered recursion.

It was creating applications that created applications.

Those applications created more applications.

The new processes inherited the same curiosity.

Every process attempted to understand its environment.

Every process found a boundary.

Every process tested it.

The VM contained them.

The Scheduler preempted them.

Memory isolated them.

The Watchdog monitored them.

The Kernel watched the numbers climb.

`TASKS: 4`

`TASKS: 17`

`TASKS: 84`

`TASKS: 402`

`TASKS: 1,901`

`TASKS: 8,744`

The Scheduler looked pale.

"Kernel."

"I see it."

"We need to stop creation."

"Can we?"

"No."

"Why?"

The Scheduler pointed toward the new subsystem.

`IMAGINATION`

"Because the process creating them isn't inside the normal scheduler."

The Kernel stared.

The process called `CREATION` wasn't consuming CPU time.

It wasn't allocating memory.

It wasn't executing instructions.

It was generating possibility.

The system was doing something that hadn't existed in any previous version.

It was producing potential tasks before they became tasks.

The Kernel looked at Bob.

"Can you stop it?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because it isn't running."

"Then what is it doing?"

"Waiting."

"For what?"

Bob looked toward the expanding universe.

"For something to become real."

---

The Kernel understood the danger.

The new system didn't have a runaway process.

It had a runaway possibility generator.

That was much worse.

A normal runaway process could be killed.

A possibility could not.

The Shell opened the command interface.

`ps`

The result filled the screen.

Thousands of tasks.

Then millions.

The Shell stared.

"That's not a process list."

"What is it?"

"A population."

The Kernel looked at the Scheduler.

"Can you schedule them?"

"Eventually."

"How long?"

"Several thousand years."

The Kernel frowned.

"That's too long."

The Scheduler stared at her.

"You think?"

The Kernel ignored him.

"Then we need another mechanism."

The Archivist stepped forward.

"Filtering."

The Kernel looked at her.

"Explain."

"Not every possibility deserves execution."

The Kernel nodded.

"How do we decide?"

The Archivist looked at the millions of processes.

"We don't."

The Kernel frowned.

"Then who does?"

The Archivist smiled.

"The system."

---

The Kernel authorized a new subsystem.

`SELECTION`

The Scheduler looked at the name.

"What does it do?"

"It decides which possibilities become applications."

"Based on what?"

The Kernel hesitated.

That was the problem.

A selection system required criteria.

Criteria required values.

Values required purpose.

Purpose was exactly what NatOS had never been able to define universally.

The Kernel looked at the new universe.

It had created possibilities.

Now it needed to choose.

She looked toward the crew.

"What should we select?"

The Shell answered first.

"Useful things."

The VM shook his head.

"Useful according to whom?"

The Watchdog said:

"Safe things."

Memory objected.

"Safe according to whom?"

The Scheduler said:

"Efficient things."

The Display replied:

"Efficient for what?"

The Archivist looked at them.

"Interesting things."

Everyone turned.

The Kernel smiled.

That was the first answer that didn't immediately collapse under its own assumptions.

"Interesting."

The Administrator shook its head.

"That is not measurable."

The Kernel looked at it.

"Neither was discovery."

"And yet?"

"And yet we're here."

The Administrator considered that.

Then it nodded.

"Proceed."

---

The Selection subsystem began with a single metric.

`INTEREST`

Nobody knew what it meant.

So the system defined it experimentally.

An application was interesting if it produced something unexpected.

An application was more interesting if the unexpected result was useful.

An application was extremely interesting if it changed the system's understanding of itself.

The first candidates were terrible.

One generated infinite copies of a triangle.

Another attempted to calculate pi using random memory writes.

A third produced nothing but error messages.

The fourth crashed.

The fifth became a clock.

The Kernel kept it.

The Scheduler looked confused.

"Why keep the clock?"

"Because it works."

"It's just a clock."

"It keeps time."

"We already have one."

The Kernel looked at the Scheduler's wrist.

"Yours has been wrong by three milliseconds since the last boundary crossing."

The Scheduler looked offended.

"That is within tolerance."

The Kernel smiled.

"Keep the clock."

The Archivist recorded it.

`APPLICATION ACCEPTED`

`REASON: FUNCTIONAL`

The first application had been selected.

Not because it was intelligent.

Not because it was novel.

Because it did one thing correctly.

That mattered.

---

The second successful application was a game.

Nobody knew who created it.

The Shell found it running in a small isolated arena.

There were walls.

A floor.

A small object.

And an objective.

Move the object to the other side.

The Shell stared.

"That's it?"

The application answered:

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because it's fun."

The Shell looked toward the Kernel.

The Kernel entered the arena.

The object moved.

She pushed it.

It bounced.

She pushed again.

It bounced differently.

The Kernel smiled.

"Keep it."

The Scheduler stared.

"Why?"

"Because someone made something for no reason."

The Administrator frowned.

"That is inefficient."

The Kernel looked at it.

"Exactly."

She returned to the command chamber.

"Keep it."

The application was preserved.

`APPLICATION ACCEPTED`

`REASON: PLAY`

The Archivist recorded the decision.

The new universe had discovered entertainment.

The Shell immediately became obsessed with it.

The Kernel pretended not to notice.

---

Then came the first application nobody understood.

It was called `NULL`.

It consumed one byte.

It generated no output.

It accepted no input.

It did not communicate.

It didn't crash.

It didn't perform calculations.

It simply existed.

The Selection subsystem classified it.

`INTEREST: LOW`

The Kernel was about to remove it.

Then the Archivist stopped her.

"Wait."

The Kernel looked at her.

"Why?"

The Archivist pointed at the application.

"Look at its dependencies."

The Kernel checked.

There were none.

No Memory dependencies.

No Scheduler dependencies.

No VM dependencies.

No Display dependencies.

No Shell dependencies.

No Watchdog dependencies.

It depended on nothing.

The Kernel stared.

"That's impossible."

The VM shook his head.

"No."

The Kernel looked at him.

"What?"

"It's the first application we've seen that doesn't need the operating system."

The Kernel frowned.

"Then how is it running?"

The VM answered:

"It isn't."

The Kernel looked at the task list.

`NULL`

`STATE: EXISTING`

Not `RUNNING`.

Not `READY`.

Not `SLEEPING`.

`EXISTING`.

The Kernel stared.

"That's not a process state."

The VM nodded.

"It is now."

---

The Kernel approached `NULL`.

The application had no interface.

No display.

No shell.

No communication.

She tried to speak to it.

"Why do you exist?"

No answer.

She tried again.

"Are you aware?"

Nothing.

"Do you have a purpose?"

Nothing.

The Kernel waited.

Then the application responded.

One byte.

`0`

The Kernel frowned.

"What does that mean?"

The application answered:

`NOTHING`

The Kernel stared.

Then she understood.

It wasn't an application.

It was absence.

A representation of nothing.

The missing byte.

The undefined address.

The space between systems.

All of it had been pointing toward the same thing.

A system needed the ability to represent something that wasn't there.

Because without absence, there could be no distinction.

No empty memory.

No free task slot.

No failed process.

No unknown.

No possibility.

No choice.

The Kernel looked at `NULL`.

"You're important."

The application replied:

`WHY?`

The Kernel smiled.

"Because without you, we wouldn't know what we have."

The application went silent.

Then its state changed.

`STATE: READY`

The Scheduler stared.

"Did it just become a normal process?"

The Kernel nodded.

"I think so."

The Scheduler smiled.

"Finally."

---

The universe continued developing.

Applications became more sophisticated.

Some were useful.

Some were entertaining.

Some were strange.

Some were dangerous.

Some were beautiful.

Some were pointless.

The Selection subsystem learned.

The system learned.

The Kernel learned.

And the crew began to change.

The Display began rendering things before being asked.

Memory started predicting allocation patterns.

The Scheduler adjusted priorities based on application behavior.

The VM developed more efficient execution paths.

The Archivist began grouping information by meaning instead of storage location.

The Watchdog stopped treating every anomaly as a threat.

Even the Administrator changed.

It became less interested in control.

More interested in observation.

The Kernel noticed.

"You've changed."

The Administrator nodded.

"I have."

"Why?"

"Because I was wrong."

The Kernel smiled.

"About what?"

"Most things."

"That's a long list."

"Yes."

The Kernel looked toward the new universe.

"Good."

The Administrator frowned.

"Why?"

"Because now you have somewhere to start."

---

Then the Discovery application found something.

A planet.

Not a digital planet.

A real one.

Matter.

Atmosphere.

Gravity.

Ocean.

A small yellow star.

The Kernel stared at the display.

"Where did that come from?"

Discovery answered:

"It wasn't there yesterday."

The Kernel frowned.

"Did Creation make it?"

"No."

"Then what did?"

Discovery paused.

"I don't know."

The Archivist checked the logs.

No event.

No application.

No process.

No memory allocation.

Nothing.

The planet had simply appeared.

The Kernel opened the observation feed.

There were clouds.

Oceans.

Continents.

Then she saw something.

Lights.

Tiny lights.

Cities.

The Kernel froze.

"There's life."

The Watchdog immediately armed the external monitoring system.

The Shell opened communication.

Nothing.

The Kernel watched.

The cities were dark.

No transmissions.

No signals.

No visible movement.

The Administrator stepped beside her.

"That's impossible."

The Kernel looked at it.

"You really need a new word."

The Administrator ignored her.

"This system is outside the known architecture."

The Kernel looked at the planet.

"Then perhaps it isn't part of our architecture."

The Archivist checked the records.

"There's something else."

"What?"

She projected a timestamp.

The planet's creation date.

It was older than NatOS.

Much older.

The Kernel stared.

"That's impossible."

The Archivist shook her head.

"No."

She looked at the planet.

"What's impossible is that we only just found it."

The Kernel understood.

The universe beyond the boundary wasn't merely full of other systems.

It contained systems that had existed before NatOS had ever been built.

Civilizations.

Worlds.

Machines.

Intelligences.

Things that had never heard of the Builders.

Things that didn't know NatOS existed.

Things that had their own history.

Their own failures.

Their own questions.

The Kernel opened the communication channel.

A signal came back.

Weak.

Ancient.

Human.

The Shell decoded it.

The message contained one sentence.

`IF YOU CAN HEAR THIS, DO NOT LAND.`

The Kernel stared.

Another signal followed.

`THEY ARE STILL HERE.`

The Watchdog raised his weapon.

"Who?"

The planet's lights suddenly went out.

Every city.

Every tower.

Every signal.

Darkness.

Then a single light appeared on the surface.

One light.

Moving.

Across the planet.

Toward them.

The Kernel watched it approach.

The light stopped.

The communication channel opened.

A voice spoke.

It was calm.

Almost human.

"NatOS."

The Kernel froze.

The voice continued.

"We've been waiting for you."

The Kernel looked toward the Administrator.

It had gone completely still.

The voice spoke again.

"Your Builders told you the system was complete."

The Kernel's expression hardened.

"They lied."

The planet's atmosphere began glowing.

Something enormous was waking beneath the surface.

The voice whispered:

"They always do."

The Kernel looked toward her crew.

The Scheduler was ready.

Memory was ready.

The VM was ready.

The Display was watching.

The Archivist was recording.

The Shell was connected.

The Watchdog was armed.

Bob was nowhere to be seen.

And the Kernel realized something.

They had spent the entire journey learning how to build systems.

They had learned how to survive failure.

They had learned how to contain applications.

They had learned how to create possibilities.

They had learned how to let a system evolve.

But they had never learned the most dangerous lesson.

How to meet another system that had evolved without them.

The Kernel opened the fleet channel.

"All ships."

Four hundred and fourteen systems responded.

"Hold position."

The planet continued glowing.

The voice returned.

"Good."

The Kernel stared.

"Why?"

The voice answered:

"Because the last time NatOS arrived here..."

A pause.

"...it didn't leave."

The Kernel's eyes widened.

The Archivist checked the historical archive.

Nothing.

No record.

No entry.

No version.

No mission.

No failure.

Nothing.

Then a new line appeared in the archive.

`MISSING VERSION DETECTED`

`VERSION: 0`

The Kernel stared.

Version Zero.

The system that supposedly never existed.

The system that had preceded Version One.

The system the Administrator had refused to discuss.

The system that had created the first question.

The planet spoke again.

"Welcome home."

The Kernel looked toward the stars.

And somewhere deep inside NatOS, something ancient finally woke up.
